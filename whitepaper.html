How Even Works

A guide for clinicians to what the app collects, how it reaches its suggestions, and where its evidence stops.

What it is

  • A quick mood and stress diary on the iPhone
  • A personal comparison of those entries with sleep, activity, calendar and alarms
  • Prompts timed before moments that have been harder for that person

What it is not

  • Not a diagnostic tool or a treatment
  • Not monitored by anyone, and not a crisis service
  • Not yet studied for benefit. It is used by a small invited group.

The clinical idea

Two established ideas shape the app. Ecological momentary assessment records how someone feels in daily life, close to the moment, which reduces recall bias compared with asking at a later visit [1]. Just-in-time adaptive interventions try to offer support at the moment a person is likely to need it and able to use it, based on information that changes over time [2].

Even applies both at the scale of one person. It does not compare anyone with a population or a norm. Each suggestion comes from that person’s own earlier entries.

How a pattern is found

Even uses simple comparisons of averages. There is no machine-learning model, and no statistical test is applied. The rules are short enough to list:

QuestionRule
Is a weekday harder?Last 8 weeks. At least 3 rated check-ins on that weekday, and an average at least 0.4 points above the overall average.
Do certain events go with stress?Compares check-ins during an event or up to 90 minutes after it with all others. At least 3 in each group.
Does short sleep go with stress?Compares days after under 6.5 hours of sleep with other days. At least 3 in each group.
Do early alarms go with stress?Compares days with a first alarm before 7 AM with days with a later one. At least 3 in each group.
What goes with feeling better?Compares lower stress, better mood and more energy for sleep of 7 hours or more, 6,000 or more steps, a workout, the 2 hours after a breathing exercise, and activities such as rest, exercise or time outdoors. Needs at least 5 check-ins each way and a 0.4 point difference.
How does mood vary?Insights also compares average mood by time of day, by activity tag, for sleep of 7 hours or more, 6,000 or more steps, any workout and heart rate variability above the person’s median, and before and after calendar events. At least 3 in each group, or a time of day with at least 3 check-ins.
When is it shown?Patterns appear at a difference of 0.3 points. A prompt needs at least 0.4.

A fictional example. Suppose check-ins near work events average 3.8 and all other check-ins average 3.0. The difference of 0.8 passes the threshold if each group has at least 3 entries. The app would say that stress “tended to be” higher, with the counts available. It would not say work causes stress.

Proactive support

Notifications are optional, and the person can see each one and why it was planned. A short set of questions, offered after the first check-in, sets the first schedule, and every setting can be changed afterward. The plan covers the next 7 days, each time arrives within about 5 minutes, and it refreshes when the app is opened. If the app stays closed, the last week repeats, while nudges before specific events stop.

What the person is asked to do

  • A breathing exercise. Four rounds of four-second inhale, hold, exhale and hold, which is under 4 breaths a minute, and about a minute in all. Reviews link slow breathing with changes in heart rate variability and with relaxation, though studies are small and varied [3]. The app says to breathe normally and stop if it feels uncomfortable.
  • A sleep target. In the evening, the app works back from tomorrow’s alarm using the person’s usual recent sleep, or 8 hours when there’s too little data. That sits within the 7 to 9 hours recommended for most adults [5]. Sleep loss has been shown experimentally to heighten negative responses to mild stressors [4], which is why sleep is a central signal here.
  • Something from their own history. If workouts have tended to go with lower stress and there’s been none today, the app suggests some movement. A check-in with high stress, low mood or low energy in the last 6 hours steers the choice toward what has gone with relief from that. The wording stays at “tended to”, and the counts are one tap away.
  • A plain next step. The first screen lists up to three things to do, such as “take a minute” or “run Even Sync”, each with its reason where there is one. Until setup is finished, it shows one setup step at a time instead: add Even to the Home Screen, sign in, add and run Even Sync, and, after the first check-in, choose notifications. Each step can be put off, and checking in never waits for setup.

Safety

  • If a person rates stress 4 or 5 on at least 3 of the last 4 calendar days, counting today, the app suggests talking to someone and links to support.
  • Settings lists crisis contacts, including 911 and the 988 Lifeline, and says plainly that the app can’t respond in a crisis.
  • Nobody reviews entries. There is no clinician view and no escalation. High scores alert no one. Even should not be relied on for monitoring risk.
  • The app does not screen for depression, anxiety or any other condition, and does not advise on medication.

What limits these results

LimitWhy it matters
Very small samplesThree entries per group is enough to display a pattern, and far too few to be confident in it. Counts are shown so readers can judge.
Many comparisonsTags, event types, weekdays, sleep and alarms are all checked with no correction for multiple comparisons, so some patterns will be chance.
ConfoundingShort sleep, early alarms and busy days often coincide. The comparisons consider one factor at a time.
Direction of effectExercise may lift mood, or feeling well may make exercise more likely. Daily totals can’t separate the two, or show whether a workout came before or after a check-in.
Who checks in, and whenPeople may record more on bad days, or stop when struggling. Prompted entries may also differ from unprompted ones. Self-monitoring can itself change mood [1].
ExpectationBeing told a hard moment is coming could raise anxiety in some people. This has not been measured in Even, and the prompts can be turned off.
Missing dataSync needs the person’s iPhone, unlocked. Calendar data covers only today and tomorrow, and old alarms aren’t kept unless a sync recorded them that day.
Wearable accuracySleep and heart measures come from consumer devices and vary by device. When two devices record the same night, overlapping time is counted once, but the devices can still disagree. Alarms that were skipped once may still appear.
Energy ratingsEnergy is only counted when someone sets it. Older check-ins hold 3 for an untouched slider, which can hide a real difference.
DeliveryiPhone settings such as Focus can hold back or hide notifications, and the app can’t tell whether one was seen.
No outcome dataEven has not been evaluated for whether it reduces stress or improves wellbeing.

Privacy

  • Mood, stress and notes stay on the device unless the person turns on check-in sync. With it on, they’re stored in the account as readable text, and erasing synced data removes them and turns sync off. Reflections always stay on the device. If the app is deleted with sync off and no backup, this data is gone.
  • Synced records are limited to the verified account that owns them, and each person can erase them or delete their account from the app. Removing a tester deletes their synced data, and devices unused for 90 days disconnect.
  • Data travels over encrypted connections. It is not end-to-end encrypted, so the service’s operators could technically access synced records, including calendar titles, alarm names and any synced check-ins and notes.
  • A first name can appear in notifications at the start and end of the day and at hard moments, so it can show on the Lock Screen.
  • The app has no ads or analytics. It is not a covered clinical record system, and should not hold information a clinic would be required to protect.

Where a clinician’s view would help

  1. Are the thresholds too eager, and how should uncertainty be shown to a lay user?
  2. Could prompting before a predicted hard moment do harm, and for whom?
  3. Is a breathing exercise the right default, and for whom should it be avoided?
  4. Should the “talk to someone” suggestion appear sooner, or read differently?
  5. What would a small study need to measure to show real benefit?
